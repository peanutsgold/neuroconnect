Moving forward

The road to recovery

Every recovery is different. The brain can adapt and rewire itself (neuroplasticity), and progress can continue for years.

  1. 1

    Emergency care

    Stabilising breathing, blood pressure, and pressure inside the skull. Scans (CT/MRI) find the damage.

  2. 2

    Hospital & acute care

    Surgery if needed, close monitoring, and preventing complications such as infections and blood clots.

  3. 3

    Rehabilitation

    A team helps rebuild skills: physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech & language therapy, and neuropsychology.

  4. 4

    Returning home & community

    Gradual return to school, work, and hobbies with adjustments. Support groups and counselling help a lot.

Everyday tips that help

📝

Use notes, phone reminders, and a calendar for memory.

😴

Keep a regular sleep routine and take rest breaks.

🧩

Do one task at a time in a quiet place.

🚫

Avoid alcohol and drugs — they slow healing.

🚶

Gentle exercise when your doctor says it’s OK.

🥗

Eat well and stay hydrated.

⛑️

Wear helmets and seatbelts to prevent another injury.

💬

Talk to someone. You don’t have to do this alone.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Can the brain heal after an injury?

Brain cells that die usually don’t grow back, but the brain can form new connections and let healthy areas take over some jobs. This is called neuroplasticity, and rehabilitation helps it along.

How long does it take to recover from a concussion?

Most people feel better within a few weeks. Some people have symptoms for months. Return to sport, school, or work step by step, and see a doctor if symptoms last or get worse.

Why do I feel so tired all the time?

Fatigue is one of the most common effects. An injured brain works harder to do everyday tasks. Pacing yourself, planning rest breaks, and good sleep help.

Why do people say brain injury is an “invisible disability”?

Many effects — memory problems, fatigue, mood changes — can’t be seen. People may look fine but still be struggling, which can lead to misunderstanding.

How can I support a loved one with a brain injury?

Be patient, keep routines simple, give information one step at a time, and celebrate small wins. Caregivers need support too — look into carer groups and respite services.
